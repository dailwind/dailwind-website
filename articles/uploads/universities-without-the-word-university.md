The Punjab government’s decision to restore the historical college names of six public-sector universities by removing the word “University” has opened an important debate about institutional heritage, academic identity and the future of higher education in Pakistan.

Under the Public Sector Universities (Amendment) Act, 2026 (Act XXIII of 2026), published in the Punjab Gazette (Extraordinary) on 6 May 2026, six institutions have been directed to revert to their former names. Subsequently, through a notification dated 23 October 2026, the Punjab Higher Education Department communicated the revised names to the vice chancellors and rectors of public and private sector universities in Punjab.

The six institutions are: Government College Lahore; Government College Faisalabad; Government College for Women, Faisalabad; Government College for Women, Sialkot; Government Sadiq College, Bahawalpur; and Lahore College for Women, Lahore.

An important clarification in the Higher Education Department’s notification states that the mandates and functions of these institutions with regard to imparting higher education shall remain unchanged. This provision is significant because it distinguishes the change in institutional nomenclature from the continuation of their higher education responsibilities.

The decision, however, has generated considerable concern among academic stakeholders. On 7 November, Dawn reported that the vice chancellors of the six institutions had jointly requested the repeal of the amendment or, alternatively, its suspension pending an independent assessment of its consequences.

The government’s argument appears to rest primarily on the preservation of historical identity. These institutions have long and distinguished educational traditions, and their original names carry considerable cultural and emotional significance. Government College Lahore, for example, has a history extending back to 1864. Restoring such historical names may therefore be seen as an effort to reconnect institutions with their educational heritage.

This is a legitimate objective. However, an important question arises: does preserving historical identity necessarily require removing the word “University”? Historical heritage and contemporary academic status are not mutually exclusive. Indeed, names such as Government College University Lahore have historically combined both identities, recognising the institution’s origins as a college while acknowledging its subsequent evolution into a university.

International experience offers useful perspectives on this debate. Imperial College London, for instance, retains the word “College” in its familiar institutional name, yet possesses university status. Its legal position was formally established through a Supplemental Charter in 2007. Similarly, King's College London retains its historical name while exercising its own degree-awarding powers. Dartmouth College in the United States also demonstrates how an institution bearing the title “College” can offer undergraduate, postgraduate and doctoral education.

South Asia provides further examples. The Indian Institutes of Technology (IITs) and Indian Institutes of Management (IIMs) do not use the word “University” in their names. Their degree-awarding powers are established through specific legislation, including the Institutes of Technology Act and the Indian Institutes of Management Act, 2017. These institutions demonstrate that academic authority and institutional recognition do not depend exclusively on nomenclature.

Pakistan also has degree-awarding institutions whose names do not contain the word “University”. The Pakistan Institute of Engineering and Applied Sciences (PIEAS) and NFC Institute of Engineering and Technology are relevant examples. Their existence illustrates that the legal authority to award degrees can be established through an appropriate statutory and regulatory framework.

These examples, however, should not be interpreted as evidence that renaming established universities is necessarily beneficial. Each institution operates within its own legal, historical and regulatory context. The more important issue is whether the proposed change serves the long-term academic interests of the affected institutions.

A university’s name is not simply a signboard or administrative label. Over time, it becomes associated with academic programmes, research publications, graduates, international collaborations, institutional rankings and professional recognition. The six institutions have developed their contemporary university identities over more than two decades. Their names are embedded in academic publications, research databases, institutional agreements and international academic networks.

Changing these names may therefore require careful management of institutional records. Research databases such as Scopus and Web of Science, institutional repositories and academic profiles rely on accurate affiliation information. A change of name does not automatically erase existing publications or citations, but inconsistent institutional metadata could make research output more difficult to identify and aggregate. Proper coordination and continuity arrangements would be essential.

The interests of students and graduates also deserve particular attention. A graduate holding a degree issued under the former university name may later need to establish the authenticity of that qualification for postgraduate admission, employment or professional purposes abroad. Such a degree does not become invalid merely because the institution changes its name. Nevertheless, the relationship between the former and revised names must be clearly documented to avoid unnecessary difficulties in verification.

The Higher Education Commission and the provincial authorities should therefore provide an explicit clarification regarding institutional recognition, degree-awarding powers, programme accreditation and the status of existing qualifications. A reliable mechanism should also ensure that previous and revised institutional names remain properly cross-referenced in official records.

Another important concern is the process through which such a significant decision has been introduced. Universities are not merely administrative units; they are academic communities comprising faculty members, students, researchers, graduates and institutional leadership. Decisions affecting their identity and international standing should ideally involve meaningful consultation with these stakeholders.

The government’s intention to preserve historical heritage deserves recognition. However, the same objective could potentially be achieved through institutional archives, historical documentation, alumni engagement and the continued promotion of original college identities without necessarily abandoning established university nomenclature.

Ultimately, the debate should not be reduced to whether an institution can function without the word “University”. International experience clearly demonstrates that it can. The more relevant question is whether changing the names of these six institutions, after decades of university-level operation, offers a meaningful academic or administrative advantage.

Higher education policy should balance historical preservation with institutional continuity, research visibility and international recognition. If the government decides to proceed with the changes, it must ensure that students, graduates and researchers do not face avoidable complications. If reconsideration remains possible, an independent review involving the universities and relevant regulatory bodies would provide a more informed basis for decision-making.

Preserving history should not mean overlooking the academic identity that institutions have built over time. The most sensible policy is not to choose between heritage and university status, but to find a way to preserve both.
